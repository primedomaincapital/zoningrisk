The Data-Center Backlash: Zoning Is Now the Industry's Biggest Risk

On a Tuesday night in San Marcos, Texas, a public hearing ran past 2 a.m. When it ended, the city council voted 5–2 to reject a $1.5 billion data center on a 200-acre site. The developer had the land, the power study, and the tax projections. What it did not have was the room.

Prime Domain Capital Insights · November 9, 2026 · 5 min read

Data centers were supposed to be the easiest real estate in America: big flat sites, willing counties, tax revenue with almost no schoolchildren attached. In 2026 that assumption broke. GatherGov, which monitors local government records nationwide, searched nearly 99,000 records over the 12 months ending September 2026 and found roughly 2,100 jurisdictions in all 50 states reaching for at least one tool to restrict data center development. The moratorium is the most visible: it appeared in 4,269 documents and led as the primary restriction mechanism in 37 states, with heavy activity in Georgia, California, and North Carolina. Outright bans or prohibited-use designations ranked second, showing up in 2,307 documents.

The trend is accelerating. Moratorium discussions accounted for 0.4 percent of GatherGov's data center document universe in October 2025. By July 2026, that share had reached 8.7 percent. Restriction discussions are growing faster than data center discussions themselves — a signal that local opposition is shifting from reactive protest to standing policy.

The cancelled pile

The cancellations are no longer anecdotes. A Programs.com tracker of large U.S. projects, at least 100 MW or $1 billion, lists 69 cancelled since January 2024 across 26 states: 15,910 MW of planned capacity on the 26 projects that reported a size, and $165 billion of announced investment on the 32 that reported a dollar amount. The Sue Data Centers registry counts 164 cancelled or withdrawn U.S. sites, 14.1 GW, and $69.9 billion, with 102 following documented opposition. Local opposition was the cited reason in 33 of the 69 Programs.com cases, zoning or permitting in 15.

The names are specific. In Trenton, Ohio, voters banned data centers above 25 MW on November 3, 2026, with roughly 80 percent of the vote, blocking an Amazon project of about 1,000 MW. In Socorro, New Mexico, a 2 GW Green Data Centers project collapsed after the university partner walked away, and the county adopted a one-year moratorium. In Millville, New Jersey, the board of commissioners banned data centers citywide in May 2026, blocking what local coverage called the largest data-center proposal in the state at 1.4 GW. In San Marcos, the council rejected the $1.5 billion project outright. In Lacy Lakeview, Texas, a $10 billion Infrakey proposal on 520 acres advanced in May 2026 over a 3,000-signature petition and an organized opposition campaign that has since become a reference case for other towns.

States are joining the fight

New York Governor Kathy Hochul signed Executive Order 62 in July 2026, imposing the country's first statewide moratorium on facilities of 50 megawatts or more. In Texas, Governor Greg Abbott ordered an audit of every queued data center on August 3, 2026, and on September 21 ordered the state environmental agency to pause all pending and new data center permits until the audits finish, with reports expected by December 10. At least 14 states filed moratorium bills. Ohio, Illinois, and Arizona froze data center tax incentives. Maine passed a statewide moratorium that was vetoed; Vermont's governor vetoed a bill setting siting and cost rules.

The tool a town reaches for depends on what state law permits. Georgia counties can impose a moratorium with a simple resolution, and they do. Pennsylvania courts have blocked moratoriums outright, so townships there declare their existing zoning defective for failing to address data centers, triggering a 180-day halt while a rewrite proceeds. New Jersey has banned development moratoriums since 1985, pushing officials straight to eliminating the use — and producing roughly 20 restriction documents for every 100 data center records, the highest rate in the country. Texas counties hold limited zoning authority, so opposition there turns on water infrastructure and grid-cost allocation rather than permit blocks.

The counterargument: the other side of the ledger

The developers' case is not empty. Data centers pay large tax bills; in Loudoun County, Virginia, they provide 38 percent of the county's general fund. A Brookings study found a county's first large data center adds roughly 100 to 200 jobs — modest, but real, and the construction payrolls are large. Developers increasingly offer closed-loop or air cooling, their own generation, and community funds, and argue that much opposition rests on outdated information about water and power use.

There is also a demand reality that no moratorium repeals. The AI buildout is real, the power has to go somewhere, and every project blocked in one county is a project that lands in another, often with worse infrastructure and less scrutiny. A county that bans data centers does not reduce national power demand. It exports the project, and the tax revenue, to the next county over.

What this means for development risk

For developers and investors, zoning risk has moved from a line item in the pro forma to the gating factor on the deal. The entitlement timeline is no longer a permitting schedule; it is a political campaign, fought in council chambers and on ballots, against opponents who have learned to use the full toolkit — moratoriums, bans, water rules, noise limits, conditional use permits, and tax-incentive freezes. The 69 cancelled projects are the visible cost. The invisible cost is the pipeline that never gets proposed, because the developer read the room first.

The underwriting lesson is blunt: power availability and land price no longer decide where a data center goes. Community consent does, and consent is now the scarcest input in the AI infrastructure boom.
