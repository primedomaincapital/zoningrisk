What a Variance Actually Costs: Fees, Consultants, and the Hearing You Lose

The filing fee is the cheapest part. A straightforward residential variance runs $1,500 to $5,000 all-in; a commercial use variance can pass $15,000 before the hearing — and the fee is non-refundable whether you win or lose.

Prime Domain Capital Insights · November 19, 2026 · 7 min read

The fence was six inches too tall. The setback was four feet short. The homeowner needed a variance — a legal permission to break the zoning rule — and the first quote she received was $250 for the application. The final bill was $4,800. The application fee was the cheapest part of the process, and it was also the only part with a published price. Everything else — the surveyor, the attorney, the hearing, the neighbor who objected — was priced by the market, and the market does not publish a fee schedule.

A variance is the zoning code's safety valve. When a property's shape, slope, or history makes the code's dimensional rules unworkable — a lot too narrow for the required setback, a slope too steep for the height limit — the owner can ask the zoning board of appeals for relief. There are two kinds. An area variance, also called a dimensional variance, adjusts a physical requirement: a setback, a height, a lot coverage ratio. A use variance allows an entirely different activity on the property, like operating a business in a residential zone. Area variances are cheaper and easier to win. Use variances are harder, more expensive, and in some states nearly impossible: most states require the applicant to prove unnecessary hardship, which typically means showing that no permitted use of the property can yield a reasonable financial return.

A straightforward residential variance typically costs between $1,500 and $5,000 when application fees and basic professional services are included. More complex requests — especially use variances that require attorneys, environmental studies, or traffic analyses — can push total costs to $15,000 or well beyond. The range exists because every dollar in the process is set locally: the city controls the application fee, dictates which professional reports are needed, and determines how many hearings the applicant must attend. The fee schedule is the only fixed number. Everything else is a negotiation with the market.

Three jurisdictions, three fee schedules

Fort Worth, Texas, publishes one of the clearest schedules in the country. A residential variance for a homestead property costs $591. A commercial or non-homestead variance costs $886. A residential special exception is $866; a commercial special exception is $1,772. An additional variance on the same property adds $296 for a homestead, $473 for a commercial property. A sign variance is $1,182. A continuance requested by the applicant — a postponement the applicant asks for — costs $709. The hearing is held on the third Wednesday of each month, the planning department mails notice to every property owner within 300 feet at least ten days before, and the applicant or an authorized agent must be present or the board may deny the case. The fees are non-refundable.

Lower Southampton Township, Pennsylvania, charges more and demands more. A residential variance for a single lot costs $900. A commercial variance costs $3,000, plus a $300 escrow fee. An appeal of a zoning officer's decision is $900 residential, $3,000 commercial. A challenge to the validity of an ordinance is $3,500 with a $5,000 escrow. A change of zoning classification is $4,000 with a $5,000 escrow. The application requires thirteen individual packets — one original, twelve copies, plus one digital copy of the plans — and the township's instructions warn that incomplete applications will not be accepted. The board meets on the second and fourth Tuesday of each month. The applicant is advised, in the instructions, to seek an attorney: township employees are not authorized to help complete the application or explain the ordinance.

Auburn, Massachusetts, charges the least on paper. A residential variance for a one- or two-family home costs $250. A multi-family residential variance is $500. A business or industrial variance is $500. An appeal is $300. A comprehensive permit is $1,000 plus $100 per unit. But the paper fee is not the total: the applicant also pays $200 for the required newspaper publication of the hearing notice, and $2.00 per abutter for mailing — the board must mail notice to every abutting property owner, twice, by first-class mail. The hearing is held on the third Thursday of each month, and the application must be filed with the town clerk in time to meet the advertising deadlines. The cheapest filing fee in the three jurisdictions still carries the same hearing, the same notice, and the same neighbor in the room.

What the fee schedule does not include

The surveyor is the first cost after the fee. A variance application requires a survey and a site plan, and the surveyor's bill runs $500 to $2,000 for a residential lot, more for a commercial site or a lot with a slope, a stream, or a disputed boundary. The attorney is the second: a zoning attorney for a straightforward residential variance bills $2,000 to $5,000, and for a contested commercial use variance, $10,000 or more — and the attorney is effectively required in Pennsylvania, where the township will not explain its own ordinance to the applicant. The traffic study, if the board requires one, runs $3,000 to $10,000. The environmental study, if the project triggers one, runs more. The expert witness, if the hearing becomes a contest of experts, bills at the rates the market sets — and the applicant pays for the experts on both sides, in effect, because the board hears the neighbors' experts too.

The hearing itself is the risk the fee schedule cannot price. The board hears the applicant, hears the neighbors, and decides. A use variance requires a showing of unnecessary hardship, and the standard is demanding: the applicant must show that the property cannot yield a reasonable return under any permitted use, that the hardship is unique to the property and not self-created, and that the variance will not alter the character of the neighborhood. A neighbor's objection does not decide the case, but it changes it — the board that hears three neighbors object is a board that will ask harder questions, require more conditions, or continue the case to another month, at $709 a continuance in Fort Worth and at the cost of another month of carrying cost everywhere else.

The counterargument: the fee is the filter

There is a defense of the cost, and it is the same defense as the cost of the hearing itself. A variance is an exception to a law the community adopted, and the process is supposed to be difficult — the difficulty is the point. A cheap, easy variance would be a rezoning by another name, granted lot by lot without the public process a rezoning requires. The fee, the survey, the hearing, and the hardship standard are the filter that separates the lot that genuinely cannot comply from the owner who would prefer not to. The homeowner with the six-inch fence and the developer with the commercial use in a residential zone are not the same applicant, and the process treats them differently because the stakes are different.

The counter to the counter is that the filter is priced in a way that favors the applicant who can afford it. The $250 filing fee in Auburn is the same for every applicant, but the $5,000 attorney and the $10,000 traffic study are not — they are affordable to the developer and prohibitive to the homeowner. The variance process, like the rezoning process, is free to oppose and expensive to pursue, and the cost falls entirely on the person asking for permission. The neighbor who objects pays nothing. The applicant who loses pays everything, including the fee, which is non-refundable in every jurisdiction in this comparison.

What to budget before filing

Budget the fee schedule first — it is the only certain number — and then budget the professionals at twice the fee. A residential area variance in Fort Worth: $591 in fees, $1,500 to $3,000 in surveyor and attorney costs, $2,000 to $4,000 all-in. The same variance in Lower Southampton: $900 in fees, thirteen packets of plans, and a total closer to $5,000. A commercial use variance anywhere: $1,000 to $3,000 in fees, $10,000 to $15,000 in professionals and studies, and a hearing where the neighbors will be present. Ask the planning department for the complete fee schedule before filing, including continuance fees and escrow requirements — the base application fee is rarely the only check the applicant writes to the municipality. And understand the standard before paying it: if the hardship is self-created — the owner bought the lot knowing the setback — the board will deny the variance, the fee will not come back, and the cheapest variance is the one never filed.
