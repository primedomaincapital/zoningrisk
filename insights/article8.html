The Neighbor Lawsuit: When Third Parties Kill a Permitted Project

The council voted yes. The permits were issued. Then a neighbor filed suit, and the lender stopped returning calls. In California, an approved project can spend five years in court — and the delay alone can kill it.

Prime Domain Capital Insights · November 20, 2026 · 8 min read

The council voted yes. The permits were issued. The developer had the land, the financing, and the approvals. Then a neighbor filed a lawsuit, and the lender stopped returning calls. The project was a 100 percent affordable housing development in San Francisco — 2550 Irving Street, in the Sunset District, a neighborhood that had added just 26 low-income homes over the preceding decades. It spent roughly 30 months fighting litigation and repeated appeals before it could move forward. It was ultimately downsized. The delays and litigation added roughly $141,000 to the cost of each unit. The neighbor did not need to win. The neighbor needed only to file.

This is the third-party challenge, and it is the most misunderstood risk in American development. A rezoning or a permit is an approval by a government agency. A third party — a neighbor, a community group, a labor union, a competing developer — can challenge that approval in court, and the challenge itself, regardless of its merits, stops the project. The mechanism differs by state. In California it is the California Environmental Quality Act, known as CEQA, which allows any person who disagrees with an agency's decision to file a lawsuit in state court. In other states it is an administrative appeal to the zoning board, a writ of certiorari, or a direct suit alleging the approval violated the zoning code. The effect is the same everywhere: the approval is frozen, the financing is at risk, and the clock starts running on a timeline the developer does not control.

CEQA is the most studied version of the mechanism, because California has studied it more than any other state has studied anything. Jennifer Hernandez, a land use attorney at Holland & Knight, has examined every CEQA lawsuit filed in California across three studies, most recently in September 2026. Her findings are stark. CEQA trial and appellate proceedings typically take about five years to complete, and while litigation is pending, most approved projects are stalled. In 2020 alone, CEQA lawsuits sought to block approximately 48,000 approved housing units statewide — just under half of the state's total housing production that year. Across the study years 2019 to 2021, housing was the top target of CEQA lawsuits challenging agency approvals of private projects, and the lawsuits challenged housing plans allowing more than one million new units.

How the challenge works

The challenge is cheap to file and expensive to defend, and that asymmetry is the mechanism. A CEQA petitioner pays a filing fee and a lawyer. The developer pays a lawyer, an environmental consultant, and the carrying cost of the land while the case proceeds. The lender, in most cases, pays nothing — because the lender stops funding. Hernandez's studies document the consequence: the unpredictability of CEQA lawsuit outcomes has made lenders, investors, and grantors unwilling to fund projects while CEQA lawsuits remain pending, which allows petitioners to avoid the preliminary injunction process — in which they would have to persuade a judge they are likely to prevail and will suffer irreparable harm, and post a bond to cover delay damages if they lose. The lawsuit itself is the injunction. No bond is required. No showing of likely success is required. The filing is enough.

The cases that result are often not about the environment. At 469 Stevenson Street in San Francisco, a housing project became tied up in environmental appeals including disputes about shadows on a nearby plaza. Years later, the site is still a parking lot. At People's Park in Berkeley, the University of California sought to redevelop a historic but decrepit park with more than 1,000 beds of student housing, at a time when students were paying $2,000 to $3,000 per bed and some were living in their cars. The First District Court of Appeal threw out the project's environmental impact report in part because it failed to analyze noise impacts from loud student parties — near a campus surrounded by fraternity and sorority houses. It took an act of the California Legislature and a ruling by the California Supreme Court to turn it around.

The pattern is not limited to housing. SummerHill Housing Group, a Bay Area developer, has had at least six projects challenged under CEQA after receiving approvals from local jurisdictions. Some were dismissed early; others went through court. The delays ran one to three years, depending on whether the cases went through appeals. In most of them, the courts ultimately ruled in favor of the project after a thorough review. The developer's executive vice president put it plainly: in nearly all of the cases, CEQA was used as a vehicle for stalling a project already approved by the local jurisdiction. Typically one individual or a vocal minority group ends up challenging the project and using court action to stop it.

Which challenges succeed, and which are leverage

Most CEQA challenges fail on the merits. The courts rule for the project more often than not, and the SummerHill record — six challenges, most resolved in the project's favor — is typical. But failure on the merits is not failure as a tactic, because the tactic is delay, and delay works whether or not the lawsuit has merit. A project that spends three years in litigation faces new interest rates, new construction costs, new market conditions, and often a new set of approvals that have expired in the meantime. Delay can become its own reward for project opponents: delay the project long enough and it has to meet new targets, and then it is a different project, or no project at all.

The challenges that succeed on the merits tend to share a feature: a genuine procedural defect in the environmental review — a missing analysis, an inadequate study, a failure to consider an alternative. The People's Park case succeeded, temporarily, because the environmental report genuinely had not analyzed the noise impact. The challenges that are leverage plays tend to share a different feature: a petitioner whose real objection is density, traffic, or neighborhood change, expressed through an environmental claim because the environmental claim is the one the law allows anyone to file. The distinction matters for the developer's defense, because a procedural defect can be fixed with a supplemental report, while a leverage play can only be waited out or settled — and the settlement, when it comes, is usually a payment, a downsizing, or both.

Unions use the mechanism too. Labor unions employ CEQA challenges when they believe a project does not include enough union jobs, using the lawsuit to put pressure on the developer. The dynamic is the same regardless of the petitioner: the lawsuit is a negotiating instrument, and the negotiation is over something other than the environment.

The counterargument: the right to challenge is the protection

The defense of the third-party challenge is the defense of the process itself. A government approval that cannot be challenged is a government approval without accountability, and the neighbors who live next to a project have a legitimate interest in whether it was approved lawfully. Environmental review exists because projects have real impacts, and the lawsuit is the enforcement mechanism for a law that would otherwise depend entirely on the agency's good faith. The 2550 Irving Street project was downsized, but it was also built — or, at least, it moved forward — and the process that delayed it also forced the developer to answer the neighbors' questions on the record.

The counter to the counter is the record of who files and what they block. The 48,000 units challenged in 2020 were not luxury towers. They were approved housing, much of it affordable, in a state with a housing shortage the legislature has declared a crisis. The projects that survive the challenge are the ones backed by developers large enough to carry five years of litigation. The projects that do not survive are the ones that cannot — and the housing that does not get built is the housing the neighbors said they wanted, in a different place, at a different time, at a different scale. The right to challenge is real. The cost of the challenge is borne by the people who need the housing, not by the people who filed the suit.

What to underwrite before the approval

Three questions should precede any entitlement in a jurisdiction where third-party challenges are common. First, is the approval discretionary or ministerial — because a ministerial approval, one that follows objective standards with no public hearing, cannot be challenged the way a discretionary approval can, and California's recent housing laws (SB 35, SB 423, AB 2011) moved qualifying projects into the ministerial category precisely to remove the lawsuit risk. Second, is the environmental review complete and defensible — because the challenges that succeed attack the report, and a thorough report is the best defense. Third, who are the neighbors, and what do they want — because the challenge that is really about density or traffic can often be settled before it is filed, for less than the cost of defending it. The neighbor lawsuit is not a risk that appears after approval. It is a risk that is priced into the land before the application is filed, and the developer who does not price it is the developer who learns about it from the lender, after the council has already voted yes.
