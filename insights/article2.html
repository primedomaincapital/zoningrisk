Entitlement Delays: The Hidden Cost in Development Proformas

Every development proforma has a line for entitlement timeline. Almost every proforma is wrong about it.

Prime Domain Capital Insights · October 17, 2026 · 5 min read
Prime Domain Capital · Insights

Every development proforma has a line for entitlement timeline. Almost every proforma is wrong about it.

In major California and New York markets, discretionary entitlements routinely take 12 to 36 months — and 48 months is not unusual for complex rezonings with environmental review. Developers routinely underwrite 12 to 18. The gap between the underwritten timeline and the actual timeline is where IRR goes to die. This is the math of delay, and it is the least honestly modeled risk in development.

The carrying-cost arithmetic

Consider a concrete example. A developer ties up a $12 million infill site with $3 million of equity and a $9 million acquisition loan at 9% interest. Monthly carrying costs: roughly $67,500 in debt service alone, plus property taxes (~$12,500/month on a $12M assessment at California rates), insurance, and soft costs for the entitlement team — architects, land-use counsel, lobbyists, EIR consultants — running $80,000 to $150,000 per month during active entitlement.

Total monthly burn: approximately $160,000 to $230,000.

Now run the scenarios. Underwritten: 15 months to entitlement, $2.4–$3.5 million in carrying costs. Actual: 30 months, $4.8–$6.9 million. The 15-month slip costs $2.4–$3.5 million in pure carry — before accounting for anything else.

But carry is only the first-order effect. The second-order effects are worse:

  • Construction cost escalation. At 4–6% annual escalation, a $40 million construction budget grows $1.6–$2.4 million per year of delay. The budget priced at acquisition is not the budget at groundbreaking.
  • Financing expiry. Construction loan commitments and equity reservations have clocks. An 18-month delay can force a refinancing at worse terms — or a capital call nobody planned.
  • Exit cap movement. A 50-basis-point rise in exit cap rates over a two-year delay on a $60 million stabilized value destroys $5+ million in residual land value. The proforma's exit assumption quietly becomes fiction.
  • Opportunity cost. The equity trapped in a stalled entitlement is equity not deployed in the next deal. At a 20% target IRR, $3 million of trapped equity costs $600,000 per year in foregone returns.

Add it up and a two-year entitlement slip on a mid-size project routinely destroys $5–$10 million of value. On a $12 million land basis targeting a 20% IRR, that is the difference between a career-making deal and a workout.

Why timelines slip

Delays are not random. They cluster around predictable failure points:

Incomplete applications and staff churn. Planning departments in major markets are understaffed. An application deemed "incomplete" restarts the review clock, and each round of comments adds 60–90 days. Staff turnover mid-review — common — means the new planner re-reviews from scratch.

EIR scope creep. Once a project triggers a full Environmental Impact Report, the timeline belongs to the consultants and the comment letters. Each round of public comment generates new study demands — traffic, shadows, air quality, cultural resources. Eighteen months for an EIR is fast. Thirty is normal.

Political calendaring. Nothing moves in the six months before a council election. Controversial projects get continued — delayed to a future hearing — repeatedly, each continuance costing 30–60 days. Applicants who don't map the electoral calendar onto their entitlement schedule learn this the expensive way.

Litigation. As covered previously, a CEQA or SEQRA challenge adds 18–36 months even when the developer ultimately wins. The lawsuit doesn't need merit. It needs a filing fee.

How the best developers underwrite delay

Top-tier developers don't predict timelines. They structure around them:

  • Probability-weighted schedules. Instead of a single 15-month assumption, they model P50/P90 timelines — the median and the bad case — and size equity and loan terms to the P90. If the deal doesn't work at 30 months, it doesn't work.
  • Milestone-based capital. Equity is drawn in tranches tied to entitlement milestones, not deployed upfront. Earnest money goes hard in stages. The capital at risk scales with certainty.
  • Extension options, purchased early. Loan extensions and purchase-agreement extensions are cheap before you need them and ruinous after. Sophisticated sponsors buy 12-month extension options at closing as a matter of policy.
  • Parallel processing. Design development, contractor pre-qualification, and capital formation run concurrently with entitlement, not sequentially after it — compressing the post-approval timeline to recapture schedule.

The industry's honest rule, earned through repeated pain: underwrite the timeline you fear, not the timeline you're told — and structure the capital so the fear doesn't kill you.

One more discipline separates the survivors: the post-mortem. After every entitlement — won or lost — the best development shops conduct a timeline autopsy: where did the schedule slip, which assumption was wrong, what signal was missed in diligence? These get codified into the firm's underwriting manual as market-specific rules: add six months for coastal zone projects in this county, assume one continuance per hearing in that city, never trust a planner's verbal timeline without a written completeness checklist. Zoning risk compounds across a portfolio. So does the learning — but only if it's written down.
