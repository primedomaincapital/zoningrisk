The Rezoning Application That Took 41 Months: A Timeline of Entitlement Delay

Eight months for the application. Twelve for the environmental report. A hearing, a continuance, an appeal, and a lawsuit. Reconstructed stage by stage from published municipal clocks, a contested California rezoning runs 41 months — and the carrying cost is the real price.

Prime Domain Capital Insights · November 19, 2026 · 8 min read

The application is filed in January. The approval comes 41 months later, in June of the third year after. In between: a completeness review, an environmental report, two planning commission hearings, a continuance, a city council vote, an appeal, and a lawsuit filed after the council voted yes. The project is the kind California says it wants — infill homes on an underused parcel, near transit, in a city with a housing shortage. The timeline that follows is a reconstruction, built stage by stage from the published clocks of the agencies that set them — San Mateo County's fee schedule, San Francisco's entitlement data, the California Department of Housing and Community Development's statewide comparison — because no single docket tells the whole story, and the stages, added together, are the story.

Month zero is the pre-application meeting, and it is not on any statutory clock. The developer meets with planning staff, brings a site plan, and learns what the city will require: a traffic study, a shadow study, a historic review, a neighborhood meeting. The meeting is free. The studies it generates are not. In San Mateo County, a major development pre-application is required before a rezoning application can even be filed. In San Francisco, the pre-application work alone adds three to six months before the formal clock starts. The 41-month timeline starts here, in a room with no minutes and no deadline.

Months one through three are the application and the completeness review. The developer files the rezoning application with a survey, a legal description, a site plan, and a tax receipt. The county has 30 days to say whether the application is complete. If it is not — and it usually is not, the first time — the clock stops while the developer supplies the missing material. Denver's published process puts rezoning at four to six months from submission to council vote, for a simple case with no environmental review. San Mateo County puts its rezoning at 8 to 10 months, with a fee of $24,000. Forsyth County, Georgia, measured its own pending cases in November 2026 and found a median of 136 days from filing to the board vote — four and a half months — when nothing changes along the way. The qualifier is doing the work in that sentence.

The environmental report

Months four through fifteen are the environmental review, and this is where the timeline first doubles. Under the California Environmental Quality Act, a project that does not qualify for an exemption needs an initial study and a negative declaration — two to three additional months — or a full environmental impact report, which adds six to twelve months. San Mateo County's schedule says so plainly: an EIR adds 6 to 12 months to the processing time, on top of the 8 to 10 months for the rezoning itself. A rezoning that needs an EIR is therefore a 14-to-22-month process before it reaches a hearing, and that is the published schedule, not the delayed one.

The EIR is also where the project becomes visible to its opponents. The draft report is published, the comment period opens, and the neighbors who will later file the lawsuit first appear — in writing, on the record, with traffic counts and shadow diagrams of their own. The report costs the developer hundreds of thousands of dollars. It costs the opponents nothing to comment on. That asymmetry is the structural fact of the entitlement process: the applicant pays for the analysis, and anyone may use it.

The hearings

Months sixteen through twenty-four are the public hearings. The planning commission holds the first hearing, takes testimony past midnight, and makes a recommendation — approval, approval with conditions, or denial. The city council holds the second hearing and votes. In San Marcos, Texas, a public hearing on a $1.5 billion data center ran past 2 a.m. before the council voted 5–2 to reject it. In San Francisco, a University of California study of five Bay Area cities found the average time to move through discretionary approvals ranged from 10 months in Oakland to 34 months in San Jose, with San Francisco averaging 31 months. The California Department of Housing and Community Development found San Francisco has the longest timelines in the state for advancing a housing project from submittal to construction — an average of 523 days just to entitlement, against 385 days for the next slowest jurisdiction, South San Francisco — and a Terner Center analysis of 2,474 San Francisco developments found permitting times ranging from under a year to fourteen years, with little consistency across project types.

The hearing is where the timeline becomes political rather than procedural. A continuance — the commission asks for a redesign, a traffic revision, a shadow study — adds a month or two each time, and there is no limit on continuances. An appeal of the commission's recommendation to the council adds another hearing and another month. The developer's carrying cost runs through all of it: interest on the land loan, property taxes, staff time, the consultants on retainer. On a $20 million land basis at 8 percent, the interest alone is $133,000 a month. At 41 months, the carrying cost is $5.5 million — before a single unit is built, and before the lawsuit that follows the approval.

The appeal and the lawsuit

Months twenty-five through forty-one are the part of the timeline that does not appear on any agency's published schedule. The council approves the rezoning. A neighbor appeals. The appeal is heard and denied. A neighbor files a lawsuit under the California Environmental Quality Act, challenging the environmental report. The lawsuit takes about five years to complete through trial and appeal, according to Holland & Knight's Jennifer Hernandez, who has studied every CEQA lawsuit filed in California across three studies. While the litigation is pending, most approved projects are stalled — lenders will not fund a project under a CEQA challenge, because the outcome is uncertain and the delay is its own risk.

The 41-month figure in this timeline is the point at which the approval is final enough to build on — the appeal denied, the lawsuit filed but the project financed anyway, or the lawsuit settled. It is not the end of the litigation. It is the end of the entitlement. The distinction matters, because the developer's pro forma counts the months to approval, and the lender counts the months to a final, unappealable judgment. The two clocks differ by years.

The cases that anchor this timeline are real. At 2550 Irving Street in San Francisco, a 100 percent affordable housing project spent roughly 30 months fighting litigation and repeated appeals before it could move forward, was ultimately downsized, and saw delays and litigation add roughly $141,000 to the cost of each unit. At 469 Stevenson Street, also in San Francisco, environmental appeals including disputes about shadows on a nearby plaza left the site a parking lot years later. At People's Park in Berkeley, a student housing project with more than 1,000 beds saw its environmental report thrown out in part because it failed to analyze noise from loud student parties — and it took an act of the California Legislature and a ruling by the California Supreme Court to turn it around. SummerHill Housing Group, a Bay Area developer, has had at least six projects challenged under CEQA after receiving local approvals; the delays ran one to three years, and the courts ultimately ruled for the project in most of them. Winning took years. Losing was never the risk. Time was.

The counterargument: the process is the protection

The defense of the timeline is that it exists for a reason. Rezoning changes what can be built next to people's homes, and the neighbors who show up at 2 a.m. are exercising a right the law gives them. Environmental review exists because projects have real impacts — traffic, shadows, water, schools — and the report is the only document that measures them before the concrete is poured. The hearings exist because the council, not the developer, decides what the city becomes. A 41-month process that produces a project the community accepts is better than a 41-day process that produces a lawsuit.

The counter to the counter is the record. CEQA lawsuits in 2020 alone sought to block approximately 48,000 approved housing units statewide — just under half of the state's total housing production that year. Housing was the top target of CEQA lawsuits challenging agency approvals of private projects across the three study years, 2019 to 2021. The lawsuits challenged housing plans allowing more than one million new units. The process that protects the community is also the process that blocks the housing the community says it needs, and the delay is not distributed evenly: the projects that can survive 41 months are the projects backed by the largest developers, and the small builders — the ones who build the missing middle — are the ones the timeline kills first.

What the timeline costs

Price the 41 months from the pro forma, and the numbers are stark. Interest on the land loan, at 8 percent on $20 million, is $133,000 a month — $5.5 million over 41 months. Property taxes, at roughly 1.2 percent, add $20,000 a month, $820,000 over the timeline. Staff time, consultants on retainer, and the environmental report add more. The total carrying cost of the delay is $6 million to $7 million on a mid-sized project, before construction starts — and the cost is borne entirely by the applicant, because the opponents pay nothing to impose it. That is the economics of entitlement delay: the delay is free to the people who cause it, and expensive to the people who endure it. The 41 months are not a bureaucratic accident. They are the price of building in a system where anyone can stop a project, and no one has to pay for stopping it.
